"How long should my episodes be?" is one of the first questions every new podcaster asks. And the most honest answer is also the least satisfying one: it depends on your format, and on what your listeners are actually doing while they listen. There is no magic number that works for every show.

That doesn't mean the answer is pure guesswork, though. Successful shows do cluster into fairly predictable ranges, and knowing them saves you from deciding by vibes.

**The quick reference by format:**

- **A daily or news-style show:** 10 to 20 minutes. Your audience is committing to fifteen minutes a day. Don't make them choose between you and their commute.
- **An interview show (the most common):** 30 to 60 minutes. This is the sweet spot for most shows. Long enough for a real conversation, short enough that a listener can finish it in one sitting.
- **A deep-dive or documentary episode:** 60 to 90 minutes. Worth it when the topic genuinely earns that time, not just because that's how long you happened to talk.
- **A roundtable with several hosts:** 45 to 75 minutes. More voices means more tangents to rein back in.

**The truth about "too long":**

Here's what nobody tells you. The problem is rarely that an episode is too long. The problem is that it *ends up* long by accident. You invite a guest, you genuinely enjoy the conversation, and suddenly you've recorded two hours and you can't bring yourself to cut anything, because "it's all good content."

But it isn't all good content. A great episode has a shape: a promise up front, a journey in the middle, and a landing at the end. When you don't edit with intent, what you're left with is a two-hour conversation that starts three times and never quite lands. Length isn't the disease. Aimlessness is.

**On the opening minutes, do this instead:**

Here's where a lot of the "always cut the first ten minutes" advice breaks down, and I think it's worth being precise about it. There are actually two very different kinds of opening, and they should be handled differently.

One is the **lead-in that was never part of the show.** This is the warm-up you record before the interview proper actually starts. The "hi, can you hear me" checks, the waiting-for-the-guest-to-log-in small talk, the casual bit where you chat before you hit record. That's not content. It's onboarding, and it is meant to be cut. Trimming it is a clean, mechanical edit, and nobody misses it.

The other is the **genuine start of the interview.** Once you've told your guest the show is live and the conversation has actually begun, that opening is part of the episode. And if the first minute gets a real laugh, or the guest says something sharp and memorable, or your own opening lands well, you should not cut it just because it's sitting at the top. Sometimes the thing people remember best is the first thing said. Good openings aren't filler.

So the rule isn't "cut the first ten minutes." The rule is: cut the part that was never the show, and keep the part that was, even if it's early. The same logic applies to the end of an episode, by the way. A natural wrap that's actually good doesn't need a hard chop just to hit a target runtime.

**So what's the actual answer?**

It's not "x minutes." It's: the shortest version that still delivers the promise you made at the top. If you can genuinely land the idea in twenty-five minutes, a tight twenty-five-minute episode beats a padded fifty-five. Deepness is a feature. Bloat is a bug. Listeners forgive a shorter episode far more easily than they forgive a boring one, and they'll come back for a show that respects their time.
