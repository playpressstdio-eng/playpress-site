Here's a question I get a lot: why do you only work with three clients at a time?

It sounds a bit like a restaurant that only sets two tables. But the answer isn't marketing mystique. It's arithmetic.

**Quality doesn't scale the way we wish it would.**

When you hire a producer, you're not buying hours. You're buying attention. And on any given episode, that attention has to cover all of this:

1. I actually listen to the whole recording, rather than skimming a transcript.
2. I cut and shape it like it's the only show I'm responsible for, because for the time I'm working on it, it is.
3. I pull three to five hooks that could survive on their own, not three to five clips that just repeat the same five minutes.
4. I review the final files myself before they reach you. No tier of underpaid hands in between getting the credit for work I should have checked.

That last one is the whole point. In a big agency, an episode routinely passes through six or seven people, each doing a small slice, and none of them accountable for the whole result. That's how you end up with a timestamp that points to nothing, or a clip that runs eight seconds longer than the thought it was supposed to capture. Nobody caught it, because nobody owned it.

**Keeping the roster small is the same thing as keeping the standard high.**

I'd rather turn work away than turn out something that isn't right. That's not me being precious about my calendar. It's a choice to protect the one thing you're actually buying, which is a show that's been cared for end to end.

So if you're on a discovery call and you hear "I only take three shows," understand what it's really saying. It's a guarantee, in plain language. Your show is going to get one pair of eyes on it, from recording to publish, every single week. That's the trade, and I think it's a good one.
